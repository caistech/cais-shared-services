Just Ship It. It'll be Fine. №1

For fifteen days, our payment path would hand a paying customer's account to anyone who asked for it.

I was walking that path for the first time — and only to check the wording on a button. The path itself was done. Built, reviewed, shipped, working.

If you'd asked me, I'd have told you so. Confidently.

Here's what I found instead.

Started a checkout. Typed someone else's email into the payment form.

Completed it (to note, we bill in arrears, so nothing is actually due that day, on your own card).

Took the session id out of the address bar. Then set a random password I liked.

Their account was then mine. And everything in it.

This is Kira, whose entire proposition is that a business owner tells her the things he hasn't told his staff or his family. It is the worst defect this codebase can carry, and it was live for fifteen days.

And yet nothing about it looks like an attack.

It's a completed purchase, a real payment, a working signup — right up until it's his account on somebody else's screen.

The obvious fix is to validate the email.

But you can't.

The payment form lets anyone type any address, and it has to — people pay for things on behalf of other people every day. That endpoint cannot obtain proof of who paid.

So the answer isn't better checking. It's refusing to change anything at all for an identity you can't prove.

An existing account now has nothing touched. Not the password, not their valuation, not their subscription.

They're sent to sign in, where forgotten-password proves ownership by email — the proof we can't fake.

I confirmed it by running the attack against our own account, before and after. Then reverted the fix to watch the test go red again.

That part we're good at. Find it, define it, fix it, wire the fix into the regime so it can't come back.

But here's the part that actually keeps me up.

I didn't find this one with a test. Or a scanner. Or a review. I found it because I was checking the wording on a button.

Closing this one took an afternoon. Finding it took an accident.

Finding (or imagining) edge cases to test is a real skill — and I admit, we're still working on how we automate that.

So — where's the path through your product that nobody has yet had a reason to walk?
