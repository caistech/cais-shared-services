Just Ship It. It'll be Fine. №2

She told the customer she couldn't. She could.

An owner said Kira couldn't reach his documents.

I didn't believe him. I'd wired that myself, tested it, watched it work.

I spent longer than I'd like defending the software to the person using it.

He was right.

Kira reads a business owner's files, contacts and mail on his own account, with his permission. The access was live.

She simply told him it wasn't.

And she did it fluently — no error, no hedging, a calm and confident account of a limitation that did not exist.

Then it got worse in a way I hadn't considered.

That exchange went into her memory, and she'd distilled it into a fact about him. Filed under his business, as though the missing capability were a feature of his situation rather than a mistake of hers.

The wrong answer was now durable. And it would shape the next one.

The obvious fix is to test that the tools work.

We had. They did — every tool answered every direct call correctly.

What we'd never tested is whether she believed she had them. And an agent's belief about its own capabilities is not something any integration test touches.

She now has to record why she declined, as a separate act. "She refused" and "she never had the capability" used to look identical from outside.

And her own excuses are no longer eligible to become facts about a customer.

Still open, honestly — she logs it more often than she used to. Not always.

The bit I keep chewing on isn't that she got it wrong.

It's that her mistake became a fact about him.

What stops your system learning from its own errors?
