## The four traits, with receipts

### 1. He gives away authority early, and it makes the substance land harder

Self-deprecation is not decoration here — it is the load-bearing move. He undercuts himself *before*
saying the serious thing, so the serious thing arrives without a sales edge.

> "as is my want, I tend to distract people from what they're doing"
> "I'm explaining it badly at the moment"
> "I still obviously have not yet got my head fully around it"
> "I'm enjoying my last month of learning new things about all sorts of things"

**Write it as:** admit the wrong belief you held, in plain words, before you explain what was true.
Never as a humble-brag ("we were *too* rigorous"). The admission has to cost something.

### 2. He leads with the human situation. Technology arrives late or not at all

> "large cohort of retiring or wishing to be retiring business people who I know have so much latent
> value that it's probably going to just dissipate because they're not in a sellable position"

A person, a loss, and why it's happening. No product named, no capability listed. Note that even
his description of a *market* is a description of people losing something.

**Write it as:** the customer appears before the system does. Where a passage is unavoidably
technical, put a person back on screen immediately after it.

### 3. He thinks in questions and hands the room the answer

> "my job in life, I guess, is to say, that's great. How does all that can possibly come together"
> "Do we think it's useful?"
> "how would the memory governance layer work with an Experian-type company, right?"

He asserts far less often than he could. This is why an open question closing a post is authentic
rather than an engagement tactic — it is genuinely how he ends things.

**Write it as:** close on a question you actually don't know the answer to. Never "what do you
think?" — a specific one, with the reason you're stuck.

### 4. He names things that haven't got a name yet

> "They're certainly opening up and sounding like the gap is known in their heads. But they don't yet
> know what to call it to fill it."

The single best line in five thousand lines of transcript. The recurring move is: describe a thing
everyone recognises and nobody has language for, then hand them the language.

**Write it as:** one naming line per piece, maximum. It's a spice.

## Rhythm

- **Rolling and clause-stacked, not punchy.** Long sentences that accumulate, then a short one that
  lands. He does not write in fragments and should not be made to.
- **Unhurried.** He arrives at the point via the situation, not before it.
- **Australian.** Spelling (organise, realise, licence-the-noun), and the understatement that goes
  with it. "Not ideal" is doing a lot of work.
- **Verbal tics to adapt, not transcribe:** "and whatever else", "or whatever", "anyway", "I guess".
  In writing these become a comma and a shrug, not the literal words. One survivor per piece at most.

## Vocabulary

**Uses:** owner, business, build, shipped, whatever, gap, latent value, sellable, memory, harness,
the thing, honestly, genuinely, worth passing on.

**Never uses — and it is conspicuous in the transcripts that he never reaches for them:** leverage,
solution, empower, seamless, robust, cutting-edge, best-in-class, next-generation, democratise,
game-changer, "excited to announce", "we believe", "at the end of the day", "in today's landscape".

**Careful with:** "AI". He says it far less than the subject matter would predict — he says what the
thing does. Prefer the behaviour to the category.

## The "And yet." bridge

His own construction, from speech. Sits at the seam between *what happened* and *why the obvious fix
fails*, and does one of two jobs: turns back to the person, or states the paradox.

> "And yet she couldn't tell him one thing he'd told her." (person)
> "And yet nothing here is broken. Every line of it does exactly what it was written to do." (paradox)

**Rule: no more than half the pieces in a set.** It is flexible enough to fit anywhere, which is
exactly how a signature becomes a tic. Use it only where you'd genuinely pause before the next
sentence out loud.

## First consumer — the driplet (six beats)

Series: **"Just Ship It. It'll be Fine."** — *What we were sure of, what happened next, and what we changed so
it can't go the same way twice.*

| # | Beat | Budget |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Headline: hubris → deflation | ≤12 words |
| 2 | The wrong thing I believed (**not** the emotion — the belief) | ~40 words |
| 3 | The incident: what, when, who, how | ~90 words |
| 4 | The fix you'd reach for first, and why it fails | ~50 words |
| 5 | What we actually changed | ~40 words |
| 6 | The open question | ~25 words |

**Hard constraint:** beats 1 and 2 must survive above LinkedIn's "…see more" fold. A *feeling* above
the fold outperforms a technical detail above the fold.

**Audience note that governs beat 3's depth:** the reader is the DILIGENCE audience — an advisor,
broker or introducer checking whether this operator will embarrass them in front of a client — not
the end customer, and not only builders. Beat 4 stays *conceptually* technical, never
*implementation* technical ("we tested the tools worked; we never tested whether she believed she
had them" ✅ — "the query filtered on agent_id, which excludes NULL" ❌, that one goes in the
newsletter). Builder comments underneath are the credential the diligence reader actually reads.

**Sanitisation is default-deny** (Rule 3): no client, partner or non-owned product names, ever. Kira
is ours and may be named. Everything else is "one of our products".
